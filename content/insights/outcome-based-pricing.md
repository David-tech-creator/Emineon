For most of its history, professional services have been sold by the hour. The model made sense when the work was mainly human effort and the client could not easily judge the output. It makes less sense when a small team amplified by AI can produce in a week what used to take a month. If the hours fall and the price stays tied to them, the firm’s revenue falls with its own productivity. If the price stays the same while hours fall, the client is paying for effort that no longer happens.

Outcome-based pricing is the obvious answer, and the large firms are moving towards it. It is also easy to get wrong. This article sets out how we structure it, the conditions under which it works, and what a buyer should ask before agreeing to it.

## Three layers, not one model

There is no single outcome-pricing model that fits every stage of an AI programme. We use three layers, each matched to how much is known at that point.

### Fixed fee for diagnosis

At the start, nobody knows yet what the outcome will be, so it cannot be priced. What can be priced is the work of finding out: interviews, baselines, business cases, a policy and a roadmap. A fixed fee for a fixed scope removes the risk for the buyer and forces discipline on the firm. It also produces the thing that later pricing depends on: a measured baseline.

### Milestones and workflows live

For the build, we price per milestone or per workflow in production. Each milestone is defined by a test anyone can run: the eval set reaches the agreed pass rate, the system is live in the production environment, the team has been trained and is operating it. This is not yet payment for business results, but it is payment for verifiable progress rather than for time spent.

### A share of the measured result

Where the baseline is clean and the result is attributable, part of the fee can be tied to the outcome: hours saved on a process, reduction in cycle time, fewer errors reaching the client. This is where the incentives align most fully, and it is also where the most care is needed.

## When outcome pricing works

Outcome pricing works when four conditions are met. If any of them is missing, it creates more argument than alignment.

1. The baseline can be measured before the work starts, with data both sides accept.
2. The result can be attributed to the system, not to market conditions, a reorganisation or a seasonal dip.
3. The client controls the conditions for success: access to data, people and systems, and the decision to roll out.
4. The measurement period is long enough to be meaningful and short enough to settle.

Back-office workflows usually meet all four. Handling time per confirmation, per KYC review or per regulatory draft is measurable, attributable and controlled. Revenue outcomes, such as more deals closed or higher client retention, usually fail the attribution test, and we avoid pricing on them.

## How it fails

We have seen outcome-based arrangements go wrong in a few recurring ways.

- The baseline is estimated rather than measured. Months later, nobody agrees what “before” looked like.
- The metric is gameable. If the fee depends on tickets closed, tickets will be closed. Pair volume metrics with quality checks.
- Adoption is outside the firm’s control. A system that works but is not rolled out produces no measured saving. The contract must say what happens then.
- The cap is missing. Uncapped success fees create resentment when the result is large. Agree a ceiling in advance.
- Everything is at risk. A firm with its entire fee at risk will pick only the safest work. A sensible mix keeps a fixed component for the effort and a variable component for the result.

> Outcome pricing is not a discount and it is not a bet. It is an agreement about what both sides are trying to achieve, written down before the work begins.

## Why it changes the work, not just the invoice

The most important effect of outcome pricing is on behaviour. A firm paid by the hour has no reason to finish early or to build something the client can run alone. A firm paid on a workflow live has every reason to choose a feasible scope, write the eval set early, design for exceptions and train the client team, because those are the things that get a system into production.

It also changes the shape of the team. Outcome pricing rewards small, senior teams that make good decisions quickly, supported by AI and by engineering capacity that scales with the work. It penalises large teams of juniors learning on the client’s time. That is a structural shift in how consulting is organised, and it is one of the reasons we built Emineon the way we did.

## What to ask before you agree

If you are buying AI work on an outcome basis, ask these questions before signing.

- How will the baseline be measured, by whom, and over what period?
- Which metric will the fee depend on, and what quality check sits next to it?
- What is fixed and what is variable, and what is the cap on the variable part?
- What happens if we decide not to roll out a system that meets its acceptance criteria?
- Who owns the code, the prompts and the evaluation sets at the end?
- How are model and infrastructure costs handled: passed through at cost, or bundled?

A firm confident in its work will have clear answers to all of them. The answers are also a good test of whether the firm has actually put systems into production before, or only written about it.
